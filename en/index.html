  • Ways to Add Eggs to Korean Ramyeon (라면에 계란 넣는 방법, Ramyeon Egg Tips)

    Welcome back to Chelsea's K-Food Recipes! Today, we're doing something a little different. Instead of sharing a full recipe, I want to talk about one of the most important questions in Korean instant noodle cooking. How do you like your eggs in ramyeon? 🍜🥚 It sounds like such a simple question, doesn't it? But if you've ever cooked Korean ramyeon with family or friends, you probably know that everyone has their own opinion. Some people like to beat their eggs before adding them. Some crack an egg directly into the boiling soup and stir everything together. Others prefer to leave the egg completely untouched. And then there are people who don't want eggs in their ramyeon at all! Personally? I like to crack a whole egg directly into the ramyeon and leave it alone. No stirring! That way, the egg white cooks gently in the hot broth while the yolk stays soft and slightly runny. And when you finally break into that beautiful, soft yolk? Oh, it's so good! But there's another reason I love this method. When you're cooking ramyeon for several people, you can add one whole egg per person. Everyone gets their own egg. No arguing about who got more egg or who didn't get any! It's simple, fair, and delicious. Today, let's talk about a few different ways to add eggs to Korean ramyeon and why my favorite method will always be the whole, unstirred egg.

  • Korean Stuffed Pepper and Perilla Leaf Jeon (고추전과 깻잎전, Gochu Jeon and Kkaennip Jeon) Recipe

    Welcome back to Chelsea's K-Food Recipes! Today, we're celebrating Chuseok (추석, Korean Thanksgiving) with two of my absolute favorite Korean holiday dishes: Stuffed Pepper Jeon (고추전, Gochu Jeon) and Stuffed Perilla Leaf Jeon (깻잎전, Kkaennip Jeon)! 🌶️🍃 Happy Chuseok! Chuseok, also called Hangawi (한가위), is one of Korea's most important traditional holidays. It's a time when families gather, share delicious food, and celebrate the autumn harvest. And if you've ever experienced a Korean holiday meal, you probably know one thing. There's always a lot of jeon! Jeon (전) refers to Korean pan-fried dishes, usually made by coating ingredients with flour and beaten eggs before cooking them in a frying pan. There are so many different kinds. Zucchini jeon, fish jeon, mushroom jeon, meat jeon, and so many more. During Korean holidays, families often prepare large platters filled with different varieties. But if you ask me which ones I love the most? Gochu jeon and kkaennip jeon. Every single time! I love the combination of juicy, savory meat filling with the fresh flavor of peppers and the wonderful aroma of Korean perilla leaves. They're both delicious, and honestly, I could eat an entire plate of just these two. The best part is that they use the same meat filling and the same simple coating method. And here's something important that applies to almost every kind of traditional Korean egg-coated jeon. First, coat the ingredients lightly with flour. Shake off the excess flour really well. Then dip them into beaten eggs. That little step makes a huge difference! Too much flour creates a thick, gummy coating instead of the delicate golden egg layer we want. Today, I'll show you how to make both of my favorite holiday jeon using one delicious meat filling. Let's celebrate Chuseok together!

    Welcome back to Chelsea's K-Food Recipes! Today, we're making two fun and nostalgic Korean instant noodle variations: Shrimp Cracker Ramen (새우깡 라면, Saeukkang Ramyeon) and Cheese Ramen (치즈 라면, Cheese Ramyeon)! 🍜🧀🦐 If you've ever tried Shin Ramyun (신라면), you probably know that it's one of Korea's most famous instant noodles. It's spicy, savory, and incredibly satisfying. But there's one little problem. It can be pretty spicy! These days, spicy Korean noodles are popular all over the world, but not everyone enjoys that level of heat. And apparently, adding shrimp crackers or a slice of cheese to Shin Ramyun was one of those creative ways people tried to make it taste a little less spicy. I find that so interesting! Instead of simply using less seasoning, people experimented with ingredients they already had at home. A handful of Saeukkang (새우깡), Korea's famous shrimp-flavored crackers, or a slice of processed cheese could turn an ordinary bowl of spicy ramyeon into something completely different. The cheese version makes the broth creamier and richer, while the shrimp cracker version adds a fun seafood flavor and a slightly thicker texture as the crackers soften. Of course, neither addition actually removes the chili from the soup. But cheese can make the heat feel gentler, and adding shrimp crackers changes the balance of flavors. And honestly, sometimes the most memorable Korean comfort foods come from little experiments like these. Today, I'll show you both versions using a package of Shin Ramyun. They're incredibly easy, inexpensive, and perfect for those days when you're craving something warm and comforting. Let's get cooking!
