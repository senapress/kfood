🍳 Different Ways to Add Eggs to Korean Ramyeon

There isn't just one correct way to add an egg to Korean ramyeon.

Each method creates a slightly different texture and flavor.

Here are some of the most common ways people enjoy their eggs in ramyeon.

1️⃣ Beat the Egg Before Adding It

This is one of the easiest ways to add an egg to ramyeon.

Crack an egg into a small bowl and beat it with a fork or chopsticks.

When the noodles are almost cooked, slowly pour the beaten egg into the simmering broth.

You can stir gently to create soft egg ribbons throughout the soup.

The egg becomes evenly distributed, making every spoonful a little richer.

Best for: People who enjoy soft pieces of egg mixed throughout their noodles and broth.

The only downside?

If you're sharing a big pot of ramyeon, it can be difficult to divide the egg evenly between everyone.

Someone might get lots of egg, while someone else barely gets any!

2️⃣ Crack the Egg Directly Into the Pot and Stir

Another popular method is to crack an egg directly into the boiling ramyeon and stir it immediately.

This breaks up the yolk and white, mixing them into the soup.

The result is a slightly richer broth with little pieces of cooked egg throughout.

Some people love this because the egg becomes part of the soup itself.

Others prefer a clearer, spicier broth without the egg mixed in.

Best for: People who like their egg fully incorporated into the ramyeon.

Personally, this isn't my favorite method.

I want to enjoy the egg yolk separately!

3️⃣ Crack the Whole Egg Into the Pot Without Stirring

This is my favorite method! ❤️

When the noodles are nearly cooked, carefully crack a whole egg directly into the simmering broth.

Then comes the most important part.

Don't stir it!

Seriously, just leave it alone.

Let the egg sit gently in the hot broth while the white begins to set.

If the egg isn't fully submerged, spoon a little hot broth over the top to help the white cook.

For a soft, runny yolk, start checking after about 2 minutes of gentle simmering.

The exact cooking time will depend on the size of the egg, the temperature of the broth, and how much of the egg is submerged.

You want the egg white to be set while the yolk remains soft.

When you eat it, you can gently break the yolk and enjoy it with the noodles.

The rich, creamy yolk tastes amazing with spicy Korean ramyeon.

And because the egg stays whole, it feels like a delicious little treat waiting in your bowl.

Best for: People who love soft yolks and want to enjoy their eggs separately from the broth.

A quick note: Runny egg yolks are not fully cooked. If you're pregnant, immunocompromised, or otherwise at higher risk from foodborne illness, use pasteurized eggs or cook the yolk until firm.

4️⃣ Add a Fried Egg on Top

You can also cook your egg separately.

Heat a little oil in a frying pan and fry an egg until it reaches your preferred doneness.

Then place it on top of the finished ramyeon.

This method gives you more control over the texture of the egg.

You can make it sunny-side up, over-easy, or fully cooked.

It also looks beautiful when served.

Best for: People who want a perfectly cooked egg without changing the texture of the ramyeon broth.

The downside is that you need another frying pan.

And honestly, when I'm making instant noodles, I usually don't want to wash extra dishes!

5️⃣ Add a Boiled Egg

Another option is to prepare a boiled egg separately.

You can use a soft-boiled egg, a medium-boiled egg, or a fully hard-boiled egg.

Cut it in half and place it on top of the finished noodles.

This is especially nice if you want your ramyeon to look a little more like restaurant-style noodle soup.

Best for: People who enjoy neatly sliced eggs and want to prepare their toppings in advance.

It's delicious, but it does take a little extra preparation.

🥚 My Favorite Trick: One Whole Egg Per Person

Now, here's the little trick I really wanted to share today.

When you're making ramyeon for several people, add one whole egg for each person and don't stir them.

That's it!

For example, if you're making ramyeon for three people, crack three whole eggs into the pot.

Try to place them in different spots so they don't stick together.

Keep the broth at a gentle simmer rather than a rolling boil.

Avoid stirring near the eggs while they cook.

Once the whites have set, carefully divide the noodles and broth between the bowls.

Then transfer one whole egg into each bowl.

Three people, three eggs. Everyone gets one!

No one has to wonder whether they got enough egg.

No one needs to search through the noodles for little pieces of scrambled egg.

And most importantly?

No fighting over the eggs!

I think that's one of the best things about this method.

It's not just about how delicious a soft egg yolk tastes.

It's also about making sure everyone gets their fair share.

Of course, you can do the same thing for two people, four people, or however many you're cooking for, as long as your pot is large enough.

Just make sure the eggs have enough space to cook gently without breaking.

💡 Tips for Adding Whole Eggs to Ramyeon

  • ✅ Add the egg near the end: This helps prevent the yolk from becoming completely firm before the noodles are ready.
  • ✅ Don't stir after adding the egg: Stirring can break the yolk and mix it into the soup.
  • ✅ Keep the broth at a gentle simmer: A vigorous boil can break apart the egg white and make the egg lose its shape.
  • ✅ Use fresh eggs: Fresh eggs generally hold their shape better when poached directly in broth.
  • ✅ Spoon hot broth over the egg white: This helps the top cook without needing to flip or stir the egg.
  • ✅ Leave space between multiple eggs: When cooking for several people, crack the eggs into separate areas of the pot.
  • ✅ Use a large enough pot: A crowded pot makes it harder to keep the eggs intact.
  • ✅ Transfer the eggs carefully: A large spoon works well for moving each egg into its own bowl.
  • ✅ Adjust the cooking time: Cook longer for a firmer yolk or use pasteurized eggs if you prefer a soft yolk while reducing food safety risks.
