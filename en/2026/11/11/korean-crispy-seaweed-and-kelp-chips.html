Korean Crispy Seaweed and Fried Kelp (김부각과 다시마튀각, Gim Bugak and Dasima Twigak) Recipe

Welcome back to Chelsea's K-Food Recipes! Today, we're making two wonderfully crispy Korean favorites, Crispy Seaweed (김부각, Gim Bugak) and Fried Kelp (다시마튀각, Dasima Twigak)! 🌿🍘

These are the kinds of Korean snacks and side dishes that make you reach for just one more piece, even when you've already eaten plenty.

They're crispy, savory, a little salty, and absolutely delicious with a bowl of rice.

But before we start, I have a little story to share.

These days in Korea, you can easily buy ready-made gim bugak and dasima twigak at supermarkets or online. They come in convenient packages, and some are even sold as trendy snacks.

But here where we live?

We're still making them ourselves at home! ㅠ_ㅜ

Sometimes I see all those convenient Korean products and think, "Wow, people in Korea can just buy these now?"

Meanwhile, we're still standing in the kitchen, spreading rice paste on seaweed, waiting for it to dry, and frying everything ourselves.

Of course, homemade versions are delicious, and there's something special about that fresh crunch.

But I have to admit, a little convenience would be nice sometimes!

The good news is that both recipes use simple ingredients, and once everything is prepared, the frying itself is quick.

There is one important difference, though.

Gim bugak needs to be coated with rice paste and dried completely before frying, while dasima twigak can be fried directly from dried kelp.

So today, we're making both, the traditional homemade way.

Let's get started!

🍎 Ingredients

For Crispy Seaweed (김부각, Gim Bugak):

  • 5 sheets plain, unseasoned dried seaweed (김, gim)
  • 1/2 cup glutinous rice flour (찹쌀가루, chapssal garu)
  • 3/4 cup water, plus a little more if needed
  • 1/4 tsp salt
  • 1 tsp sesame seeds (optional)
  • Neutral cooking oil, for frying

For Fried Kelp (다시마튀각, Dasima Twigak):

  • 50g dried kelp (다시마, dasima)
  • Neutral cooking oil, for frying
  • 1-2 tsp sugar, or to taste
  • A small pinch of salt (optional)

Equipment:

  • A small saucepan
  • A pastry brush or spoon
  • A drying rack or parchment-lined tray
  • A deep frying pan or pot
  • Kitchen tongs or chopsticks
  • Paper towels or a wire rack for draining

👩‍🍳 Instructions

1️⃣ Prepare the Rice Paste for Gim Bugak

In a small saucepan, combine 1/2 cup glutinous rice flour, 3/4 cup water, and 1/4 teaspoon salt.

Whisk until there are no lumps.

Place the saucepan over low heat and stir continuously.

As the mixture heats, it will begin to thicken.

Continue stirring until it becomes a smooth, sticky paste that can be spread easily.

If it becomes too thick, add a little more water.

Remove from the heat and let it cool until it's comfortable to handle.

This rice paste is what gives traditional gim bugak its wonderfully light, crispy coating.

2️⃣ Coat the Seaweed

Place a sheet of plain dried seaweed on a clean, flat surface.

Using a pastry brush or the back of a spoon, spread a thin, even layer of rice paste over the seaweed.

Don't apply too much.

A thin coating helps the seaweed dry evenly and become crispy when fried.

If you'd like, sprinkle a few sesame seeds over the paste.

You can leave the seaweed as a single sheet or fold it in half with the paste inside for a thicker version.

Repeat with the remaining seaweed sheets.

3️⃣ Dry the Coated Seaweed Completely

Arrange the coated seaweed on a drying rack or parchment-lined tray.

Let it dry until the rice paste is completely hard and no longer sticky.

Traditionally, gim bugak is dried in the sun or in a well-ventilated area.

Depending on the temperature and humidity, this may take a day or longer.

For a more convenient method, use a food dehydrator or an oven on its lowest suitable temperature, around 50-60°C (120-140°F), with good airflow.

Drying time will vary depending on the thickness of the coating and your equipment.

This step is extremely important!

If the rice paste is still damp, the seaweed may splatter when fried and won't become properly crispy.

Once completely dry, cut the sheets into bite-sized pieces using kitchen scissors.

4️⃣ Fry the Gim Bugak

Pour enough neutral cooking oil into a deep frying pan or pot for shallow or deep frying.

Heat the oil to approximately 170-180°C (340-355°F).

Carefully add a few pieces of dried, coated seaweed at a time.

They should puff up and become crispy very quickly, usually within a few seconds.

Turn them gently if needed, then remove them as soon as they're crisp.

Don't leave them in the oil too long because seaweed burns easily.

Transfer the fried gim bugak to a wire rack or paper towel to drain excess oil.

Repeat with the remaining pieces.

Your homemade gim bugak is ready!

5️⃣ Prepare the Dried Kelp

Now let's make Dasima Twigak (다시마튀각)!

Unlike gim bugak, fried kelp is much easier to prepare.

There's no rice paste and no long drying process.

Start with completely dried kelp.

If the kelp has visible dust or debris, gently wipe it with a dry cloth.

Do not soak or rinse the kelp with water.

The kelp must remain dry before frying.

Using kitchen scissors, cut the kelp into small, bite-sized rectangles.

Remember that the pieces may expand slightly as they fry.

6️⃣ Fry the Kelp

Heat clean neutral cooking oil in a frying pan or pot to approximately 170°C (340°F).

Carefully add a few pieces of dried kelp.

The kelp will quickly puff, curl, and become crispy.

This usually takes only a few seconds.

Remove the pieces promptly before they become too dark or bitter.

Transfer to a wire rack or paper towel to drain.

Continue frying the remaining kelp in small batches.

7️⃣ Add Sugar to the Fried Kelp

While the fried kelp is still slightly warm, sprinkle 1-2 teaspoons of sugar over it.

Toss gently to distribute the sugar.

Some Korean families prefer a sweeter version, while others like it with just a little sugar or no sugar at all.

Taste and adjust according to your preference.

If needed, add a tiny pinch of salt.

The combination of crispy kelp and a light sugar coating creates that familiar sweet-and-savory flavor.

8️⃣ Serve and Enjoy

Arrange the crispy gim bugak and dasima twigak on separate plates.

Serve them as Korean side dishes alongside rice and soup, or enjoy them as crunchy snacks.

Gim bugak has a delicate, airy crunch with a nutty rice coating.

Dasima twigak is crisp, savory, and slightly sweet.

Both are delicious freshly fried!

Let them cool completely before storing in airtight containers.

Keep them in a cool, dry place and enjoy them while they're still crisp.

💡 Tips for Perfect Gim Bugak and Dasima Twigak

  • ✅ Use plain, unseasoned seaweed: Regular dried gim works best for gim bugak. Pre-roasted and heavily seasoned snack seaweed is not ideal.
  • ✅ Dry the rice paste completely: This is the most important step for crispy gim bugak. Any remaining moisture can cause dangerous oil splattering.
  • ✅ Spread the paste thinly: A thick layer takes much longer to dry and may result in an uneven texture.
  • ✅ Fry in small batches: Both seaweed and kelp cook extremely quickly, so avoid overcrowding the pan.
  • ✅ Watch the oil temperature: Oil that's too cool can make the snacks greasy, while oil that's too hot can burn them almost instantly.
  • ✅ Never soak the kelp: Dasima twigak is made with dry kelp. Moisture and hot oil are a dangerous combination.
  • ✅ Adjust the sweetness: Fried kelp is often enjoyed with sugar, but you can use less or skip it entirely.
  • ✅ Store only after cooling: Any trapped steam can make the finished snacks soggy.
  • ✅ Make extra dried gim bugak: Once the coated seaweed has dried completely, you can store it in an airtight container and fry small batches whenever you're craving something crispy.

Gim Bugak (김부각) and Dasima Twigak (다시마튀각) are wonderful examples of traditional Korean food made from simple ingredients.

Seaweed, kelp, rice flour, and a little oil.

That's really all it takes.

But there's something about the crispy texture and savory flavor that makes these snacks so irresistible.

In Korea, many traditional foods that once required a lot of preparation are now available as convenient ready-made products.

And honestly, I'm a little jealous sometimes!

When I see packaged gim bugak at Korean supermarkets, I can't help thinking about how much easier life has become.

Meanwhile, we're still making it the old-fashioned way here.

Spreading rice paste, waiting for the seaweed to dry, heating the oil, and frying each piece carefully.

Sometimes I just want to open a bag and eat it, too! ㅠ_ㅜ

But at the same time, there's something special about making these foods at home.

Freshly fried gim bugak has a light, delicate crunch that's hard to resist.

And homemade dasima twigak, with its sweet and savory flavor, always reminds me of traditional Korean side dishes.

These are the kinds of foods that connect everyday cooking with childhood memories and family traditions.

Even when the process takes a little extra effort, the result is worth it.

So if you live somewhere where ready-made Korean snacks aren't always easy to find, don't worry.

With a few simple ingredients and a little patience, you can still enjoy these delicious Korean classics at home.

And maybe someday we'll just buy a bag from the store like everyone in Korea does!

Until then, homemade gim bugak and dasima twigak it is.
